An authentic jewelry company wanted to expand from offline retail into a digital product. I joined as the product designer, studied the business context, customer behavior and competitors, and translated the findings into the first mobile app experience.

We developed an individual plan together with the business customer: clarified the brief, described the constraints, collected materials from the team and defined the MVP scope for iOS and Android.

Team

Project stakeholders included the IT Director, a Marketing Specialist, a Category Manager, and Retail Point Administrators. The design phase lasted 45 days.

Segments

how did you segment it?

The segments are built at the intersection of CRM downloads for 60+ retail outlets and systematic observations of store administrators. The life situation is chosen as an axis - it determines the purchase scenario more than demographics.

JTBD

For each MVP segment, three layers of jobs are formulated: the main one - why it comes, the secondary one - what it does if you didn’t buy it right away, the latent one - the hidden fear that kills conversion. Each latent job is mapped to a specific UX pattern in the interface.

Which segments did we prioritize?

Self-purchase is the mass segment and immediate revenue. Gift buyers are a quick win around holidays. Zoomers support audience growth. Collectors and “about” scenarios were moved to the backlog.

Benchmarks

methodology

6 products: Oskelly, Poison Drop, Farfetch, Lichi, Golden Apple, Lamoda. Compare on 7 axes: home, catalog and filters, product card, support and cart, profile, favorites, service settings. For each - what works well, what doesn’t, what to spy on.

What patterns did I bring into the product?

Hero banner on the main page instead of the product grid (Lamoda, Farfetch). Thematic story collections with social network mechanics (Lamoda, Lichi). Support chat in the context of the catalog and checkout (Poison Drop). Hint favorites inside the card without leaving the page (Farfetch).

Scoping

scoping

14 product hypotheses for AARRR linked to metrics. Each one is in the format “if X, then Y, because Z” - without which it is impossible to evaluate impact and confidence.

Hypotheses by direction

All hypotheses are assessed using ICE: impact, confidence, ease. Top priorities: scenario-based entry (432), three steps before purchase (320), live photo (270), warm wishlist (256), gift in 5 minutes (240).

what changed after the meeting with stakeholders?

After a meeting with stakeholders, the launch was divided into two releases. R1 - basic. R2 - gift script and validation of Track B. Some of the hypotheses went into the backlog of feedback from IT, commercial and marketing specialists.

Screens

Home: entry point into the product

The first thing the user sees is a hero banner with a promotion. Not a product grid, but an emotional photograph of jewelry on a model. Below are stories with mechanics from social networks: “new items”, “sale%”, “bestsellers”.

catalog and navigation

The catalog begins with a clean list of categories - large text, without visual noise. Solution from the “anti-dead-end catalog” hypothesis: first we narrow the selection, then we show the results. The search works with hints: entering “gold” returns “gold 585”, “gold ring”.

filters

Filters remain in the search context: the user specifies the material, size, price and availability without losing the selected category. Once applied, the catalog leads directly back to the products, maintaining the feeling of a short path to purchase.

Product card: emotion + ratio

The card is the place of the final decision. Show beauty, give characteristics, remove doubts, push to purchase.

cart and checkout

At the top of the basket is a summary: amount, discount, total. The buyer sees the benefit before scrolling. The checkout is divided into 4 steps according to the principle of “one decision per screen” - this is “Three steps to purchase” from R1, hypothesis No. 2 for ICE. Baymard: every extra field in the form = -7% conversion.

U-test

task

The screens are designed - but do they work? I conducted a series of tests with real users from target segments. Goal: Check if the user can find the product and place an order without prompting.

legend

“You are going to buy a 585 gold ring for yourself (or a loved one). Try to place your order as quickly as possible."

scale

Excellent - from the first click, Good - not right away, but figured it out, Negative - didn’t figure it out, Didn’t do it - alternative way.

results

92% of tasks (37 out of 40) were completed with an “Excellent” rating - from the first click. Not a single “Negative” rating. 100% of respondents completed the order.

What has changed?

after the test

I clarified the microcopywriting in the shopping cart, strengthened the hints in the catalog and left the product card calmer: the visual sells the jewelry, and the characteristics and conditions help remove doubts without overload.

Results

final

After usability testing and iteration, the project was transferred to development as an app for two platforms: iOS and Android.

what was confirmed

The scenario-based entry, short catalog, large product card and simple checkout stood the test with real users and remained in the MVP.
